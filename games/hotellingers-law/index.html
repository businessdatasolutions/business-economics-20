About Hotelling's Law Simulation
What is this Simulation?

This model is a representation of Hotelling's law (1929), which examines the optimal placement of stores and pricing of their goods to maximize profit. In Hotelling's original paper, stores were confined to a single dimension (a line). This model replicates and extends this by allowing stores to move freely on a plane.

How the Simulation Works

Several stores attempt to maximize their profits by moving and changing their prices. Each consumer (represented by grid points on the canvas) chooses their preferred store based on a combination of the distance to the store and the price of its goods – they pick the one with the lowest (distance + price) sum. If there's a tie, they choose randomly.

Stores make decisions without knowing their competitors' immediate plans. They try potential moves (North, South, East, West, or stay) to see if they can gain more market share. Then, they check if increasing or decreasing their price will lead to greater profit (price × market share). They adopt the change if it's beneficial.

Understanding Hotelling's Law: The Ice Cream Vendor Analogy

Harold Hotelling, an economist trained in mathematics, made profound observations about competition. His law can be illustrated with a simple example:

Imagine a mile-long beach where two vendors want to sell identical ice cream at the same price. Sunbathers will naturally go to the closest cart. Where should the vendors position themselves to get the most customers?

  • An "Optimal" Start: If we were planning for the sunbathers, we might place the carts a quarter of a mile from opposite ends. This way, no one walks more than a quarter-mile.
  • The Vendor's Dilemma: Now, consider one vendor. They serve half the beach. If they move slightly towards the center, they keep all their current customers (they're still closer) AND capture some from the middle who were previously indifferent or closer to the competitor. The closer they move to their competitor, the more customers they might poach.
  • The Competitor Reacts: The other vendor, seeing this, will do the same.
  • The Equilibrium: This continues until both vendors are side-by-side right in the middle of the beach. At this point, neither can improve their situation by moving. This is an equilibrium.
  • The Consequence: The vendors haven't gained more customers overall (still roughly half each), but many sunbathers now have to walk much further – up to half a mile!

Hotelling's Law states there's a natural tendency for competitors to be pulled toward a common middle ground, even if it's not optimal for consumers.

Real-World Examples

This "pull to the middle" can be seen in many areas:

  • Product Similarity: Refrigerators or other appliances in the same price range often look and function very similarly.
  • Business Clusters: Car dealerships or fast-food restaurants often group together on the same stretch of road.
  • Politics: Political candidates often shift their positions towards the center to capture undecided voters, sometimes making their platforms less distinct.

How to Use This Simulation
  • Setup: Creates the stores and visualizes their starting market areas. Use this after changing the Number of Stores.
  • Go / Pause: Starts or stops the continuous simulation.
  • Go Once: Runs the simulation for a single step (tick).
  • Number of Stores: Sets how many competitors are in the world (requires Setup).
  • Layout: Choose 'Line' (stores on a horizontal line) or 'Plane' (stores move freely).
  • Rules:
    • Normal: Stores can both move and change prices.
    • Price Only: Stores can only change prices.
    • Move Only: Stores can only move.
  • Simulation Speed: Adjusts how many simulation ticks happen per second.
Things to Notice in the Simulation

With default settings (2 stores, plane, normal), notice how stores often end up very close to each other (clustering) and with low prices. This is a classic Hotelling's law result – competition for fringe customers pulls them together.

The boundaries between store areas can look like lines or curves (hyperbolic arcs), depending on price differences.
